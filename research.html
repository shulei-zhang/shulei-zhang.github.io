Job Market Paper

When Primary Care Collapses: Rural Economic Reform and Rising Mortality in China

With Minhee Chae and Xin Meng · Job Market Paper

China’s agricultural decollectivization, carried out between 1979 and 1984, replaced commune-based farming with household production and substantially raised productivity. It also dismantled the collective institutions that financed and organised rural public services. This paper examines the consequences of this institutional transformation for rural health.

Using a newly digitized county-by-year dataset that links mortality, disease incidence, and local health provision, and staggered difference-in-differences, we find that county mortality rates increased by roughly 1.6 percent following the reform, implying approximately 90,000 excess deaths annually nationwide. The mortality increase is concentrated in counties with the largest declines in village-doctor availability (the so-called “barefoot doctors”) and is accompanied by a higher probability of local infectious-disease surges after the reform.

These patterns are not explained by changes in government health expenditures, and accounting for differential pre-reform income trajectories modestly increases the estimated effect. Thus, rural market-oriented reforms carried substantial and long-lasting health costs by weakening local primary care capacity.

Working Papers

Carrots and Sticks: Decoding China’s One-Child Policy with Large Language Models

With Xin Meng and Sen Xue

This paper studies how local governments translated China’s common fertility-control objective into heterogeneous policy instruments. Using large-language-model (LLM)-assisted analysis of county gazetteers, we construct new county-level measures of the types and intensity of rewards and penalties specified in local family-planning policies. This allows us to move beyond a single measure of One-Child Policy exposure and examine how local governments translated a common national objective into different policy designs, and how variation in those designs relates to fertility behavior. When these instruments are considered jointly, non-monetary penalties—primarily administrative sanctions—are most strongly associated with fertility decline, with a significantly stronger relationship in urban than in rural areas.

A new investigation on Land Reform, One-Child Policy and Sex Selection in China

With Xin Meng and Sen Xue

Building on the pioneering work of Almond et al. (2019), we revisit the relationship between the Household Responsibility System, the One-Child Policy, and sex selection in rural China. Using an expanded county sample, we reconstruct local exposure to both reforms and distinguish pilot and broader adoption stages. The new measures reveal a different pattern: the effects are concentrated among households exposed to both policies. The findings suggest that economic resources and fertility constraints interacted to intensify households’ sex-selective behavior. We further find evidence that the pilot and broader implementation phases of each reform had differential impacts on fertility outcomes, reflecting evolving mechanisms as the two policies progressed.
