When Primary Care Collapses: Rural Economic Reform and Rising Mortality in China

With Minhee Chae and Xin Meng · Job Market Paper

Presented at: LEW 2026 · ACE 2026 · CCER Summer Institute 2026 · AEDC 2026 · ADEW 2026

We study a hidden public health cost of China’s agricultural decollectivization. Using a new county-by-year panel assembled from more than 3,000 local gazetteers and provincial statistical records, we link the staggered introduction of the Household Responsibility System to mortality, infectious disease, village-level medical personnel, fiscal spending, and local economic conditions.

County mortality rises by about 1.6 percent after reform, implying roughly 90,000 excess deaths annually at the national scale. The increase is concentrated in counties with the largest declines in village-doctor availability, while post-reform income gains modestly offset the deterioration in health. Several infectious diseases show breaks in their pre-reform downward trends, while local disease surges become more frequent.

Carrots and Sticks: Decoding China’s One-Child Policy with Large Language Models

With Xin Meng and Sen Xue

We study how local governments translated China’s nationwide fertility-control objective into different combinations of rewards and penalties. Using large-language-model (LLM)-assisted analysis of county gazetteers, we construct new measures of the types and intensity of local policy instruments. We find that non-monetary penalties—primarily administrative sanctions—are most strongly associated with fertility decline, with a significantly larger relationship in urban than in rural areas.

A new investigation on Land Reform, OCP and Sex Selection in China

With Xin Meng and Sen Xue

Presented at: RSE PhD Workshop · RSE Seminar

Building on Almond et al. (2019), we revisit how the Household Responsibility System and the One-Child Policy jointly shaped sex selection in rural China. Using an expanded county sample and newly reconstructed policy timing that distinguishes pilot and broader adoption stages, we find that the two reforms matter primarily through their joint exposure, suggesting that economic resources and fertility constraints interacted to intensify households’ sex-selective behavior.
